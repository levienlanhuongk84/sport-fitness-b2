"What would you ask this patient first?"
Gợi ý trả lời

How long have you had the pain? Do you sit for long periods every day? Do you get any exercise during the week?

Người chạy bộ, đau đầu gối sau khi tập
"What advice would you give about their training?"
Gợi ý trả lời

Rest for a few days, ice the knee, and avoid running on hard surfaces. Build up your distance gradually next time.

Người cao tuổi, cứng khớp, ít vận động
"What kind of physical activity is safe for them?"
Gợi ý trả lời

Low-impact activities like walking, swimming or gentle stretching are safer than running or jumping.

Thừa cân, nguy cơ tiểu đường và tim mạch
"How would you explain the benefits of exercise?"
Gợi ý trả lời

Regular exercise lowers blood pressure, controls blood sugar and reduces the risk of heart disease — even 30 minutes of walking a day helps.

Bác sĩ / điều dưỡng trực đêm, không có thời gian tập
"What would you say about your own exercise routine?"
Gợi ý trả lời

I try to fit in short walks or a quick home workout between shifts, even if it's only 15–20 minutes.

Bác sĩ kê "đơn vận động" thay vì chỉ kê thuốc
"How do you usually recommend exercise to a patient?"
Gợi ý trả lời

I usually recommend starting small — walking three times a week — and building up gradually depending on the patient's condition.

Bài mẫu Task 2 (≈ 290 từ)

Across the world, levels of physical activity have fallen sharply in recent decades, and sedentary lifestyles are now a major public health concern. This essay will examine the main causes of this trend and suggest some measures that could reverse it.

The most obvious cause is the nature of modern work. Millions of people spend eight hours a day sitting at a desk, and after a long commute they have neither the time nor the energy to exercise. Technology has made the problem worse: leisure time that was once spent outdoors is now spent in front of a screen. In addition, in many cities there is a shortage of safe, affordable sports facilities, so even those who want to keep fit find it difficult to do so.

Several measures could encourage people to become more active. Firstly, governments should invest in free or low‑cost facilities such as parks, cycle lanes and public swimming pools, since cost and access are major barriers. Secondly, employers could play a role by offering flexible hours or gym membership, which would allow staff to fit exercise into a busy schedule. Finally, doctors should treat physical activity as a form of medicine and give patients clear, realistic advice — for instance, 150 minutes of moderate exercise a week — rather than simply telling them to "do more sport".

In conclusion, inactivity is largely the result of sedentary jobs, screen‑based leisure and poor access to facilities. However, with investment from governments, support from employers and practical guidance from health professionals, this trend can be reversed, and the benefits — for both individuals and the health system — would be considerable.
